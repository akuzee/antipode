The Circle at Your Feet

If the ground were a glass window — a circle of radius r centered at your feet, Earth transparent below — how much of the far side of the planet could you see through it?

To make it physical: drop a hula hoop on the ground around your feet. Standing in New York, through that hoop you'd see a bit of Australia and a sliver of Antarctica, across a patch of the Indian Ocean. Stand in the center circle of a basketball court and the window shows more than half the planet.

And if "everything is down" sounds too strong: of course, a lot of your own continent is more "next to you" than "under you". But curvature kicks in faster than you'd think. Stand in New York and look directly at Los Angeles — the actual straight line to the city, not the direction you'd drive — and you're looking at a spot on the pavement about 17 feet away from you.

Why the window shows double its angle — and why walking around changes nothing

Θ = 2ν. Your sight line is a chord of the sphere. A chord leaving you at angle ν from the vertical diameter (the line through you, the center, and your antipode) lands on the far surface at arc distance 2ν from the antipode — the inscribed angle theorem: an angle with its vertex on a circle subtends twice itself at the center. So a window spanning ν = 45° to each side (radius = eye height) shows a cap of Θ = 90°, which is exactly half the Earth's surface: a circle as wide as you are tall shows you half the planet.

Position doesn't matter, only the window. The far surface is ~12,742 km away; stepping 10 m sideways changes the direction to any far-side point by less than an arcsecond. The visible cap is pinned by the window's angular size alone — which is why the companion AR app needs no positional tracking at all.

The map is mirrored. You are reading the far surface from the inside, so east and west swap relative to a wall map. Perth sits northeast of New York's antipode but appears to the west. That is correct, and the map above shares the convention of the AR app.
